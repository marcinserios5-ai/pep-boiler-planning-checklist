One project brief for boiler installation and underfloor heating in London

Describe the finished home before choosing equipment

A London refurbishment can combine a boiler installation with underfloor heating, changes to hot-water storage and work in a plant room. Those decisions affect one another, so begin with a description of the finished home rather than a shopping list of appliances. Explain which rooms are changing, which spaces will remain occupied and what the household wants the completed project to provide.

Keep observed conditions separate from preferences. Record the existing arrangement as accurately as you can without opening equipment or disturbing services. Then describe the changes you would like assessed. The contractor needs to establish the appropriate technical solution for the actual property; an early homeowner brief should make the questions clear without attempting to specify an unassessed installation.

Make the boiler and floor-heating discussions meet

If a boiler replacement and underfloor heating form part of the same project, tell the people assessing each part from the outset. Ask who will coordinate the proposed heat source, retained heating, floor construction and controls. A quotation for one item should not silently become a promise to design, alter or commission every connected part of the home.

Give the contractor current floor plans and the proposed room uses where those are available. State whether floor finishes, door positions or fixed furniture remain provisional. Ask which decisions need to be settled before the installation proposal can be completed. Record the answer alongside the relevant drawing so a later change can be checked against the agreed basis.

Put hot water and plant space into the same brief

Describe how the household expects to use hot water and mention planned changes to bathrooms. If an unvented cylinder is being considered, ask the appropriate professional to assess the property and proposed arrangement. Do not assume that changing a cylinder alone guarantees an improvement, or that another property’s equipment provides a suitable specification for this home.

Where equipment is grouped in a plant room or cupboard, discuss its role in the wider project. Ask how the proposed layout will be assessed for access, maintenance and coordination with other trades. Keep cupboard dimensions and storage ideas visibly provisional until the relevant requirements have been established. Space allocated on a drawing is not evidence that an installation is suitable.

Turn interfaces into named responsibilities

Identify who coordinates the heating work, electrical interfaces, floor preparation and finished surfaces. For every boundary, ask what each contractor includes and what must be supplied or completed by someone else. Keep the answers in a short responsibility schedule. A task described as by others still needs an identified owner and an agreed place in the programme.

Use the same dated brief when requesting proposals. Retain assumptions, exclusions and unanswered questions with each quotation rather than comparing totals alone. If the scope changes, ask for its effect on the programme and price before treating the change as agreed. Share the current version with the relevant people so instructions do not drift between conversations.

Agree what completion will demonstrate

Before work begins, ask how the agreed installation will be checked and handed over. Record the documents, operating explanation and follow-up contact that belong to the accepted scope. Where several systems are involved, clarify who confirms the interfaces between them. Technical testing and commissioning belong with the competent professionals responsible for the work, rather than a homeowner’s improvised checklist.

Keep the final agreed brief, quotations and changes together with the handover information. They provide context if a question arises after completion or a later project affects the installation. Review the service information below for the relevant parts of a London enquiry, then arrange an assessment based on the actual property. The written scope should explain the project that will be delivered.

PEP · HOMEOWNER PLANNING GUIDE

Living at home during a boiler installation in Queen’s Park: agree a daily plan

Discuss the occupied-home arrangements

When a household stays at home during boiler work, a practical daily plan helps everyone understand what to expect. Discuss the occupied-home arrangements with the contractor before fixing the start. Explain normal routines, the spaces people need to use and any times when access to a particular room will be difficult.

For a Queen’s Park home, base the discussion on the actual layout and proposed scope. Do not assume that another installation’s timetable will apply to your property. The contractor needs to explain the sequence for this job, including which arrangements can be confirmed and which depend on information still being checked.

Ask about planned interruptions

Ask the installer which services may be interrupted during the proposed work and how the household will be told before an interruption begins. Record the contractor’s expected sequence without treating an estimate as a guarantee. Keep a clear contact route for questions if the programme changes or an arrangement needs to be reconsidered.

Discuss the household’s needs directly rather than improvising technical solutions. Ask the appropriate contractor what arrangements are suitable for the circumstances and who is responsible for them. A homeowner’s project plan should explain agreed practical arrangements; it should not become instructions for connecting, isolating, adjusting or repairing any equipment.

Define the working area

Walk through the proposed working area and access route with the installer. Agree what the household should clear in advance, what protection is included and which spaces remain available for ordinary use. Keep the distinction between the contractor’s work area and the rest of the home clear for everyone who will be present.

Where belongings need to be moved, identify who will move them and where they can be kept without obstructing access. Avoid assuming that an installer will pack cupboards, move delicate possessions or arrange storage unless that has been agreed. Record any task that belongs to the homeowner or a separate contractor before the start.

Coordinate work and household commitments

If people work from home or have appointments that require a quiet room, mention the relevant timing before the programme is agreed. Ask which activities are expected to be disruptive and whether a different arrangement is needed. Keep provisional timings visibly provisional, so the household does not build an important commitment around an unconfirmed assumption.

Where other trades will attend, share the agreed sequence and identify who coordinates changes. Confirm who can provide access if the main household contact is away. A short daily contact arrangement is often easier to use than several separate messages that leave different people working from different versions of the plan.

Review the next day before it starts

At the agreed review point, ask what has been completed, what is expected next and whether the household needs to prepare anything differently. Note a change against the current programme and identify the person responsible for the next action. Keep unanswered questions open until the relevant contractor has responded, rather than assuming silence confirms an arrangement.

Retain the final programme notes with the accepted quotation and handover information. They help explain what was agreed if a question arises later. Leave technical assessment and gas work with appropriately qualified professionals; HSE explains how to check an engineer. The household’s role is to provide accurate information and agree workable access and communication arrangements.

A practical checklist before replacing a boiler

A useful quotation starts with a clear brief. This checklist helps you collect information for a site visit, agree how the work will fit around your household and compare what each quotation includes. It is a preparation aid: the installer still needs to assess the property and specify the installation.

0 of 9 points noted
Tick a point when you have recorded it for your project.

Prepare the brief

Include the route to the work area. Photographs help with preparation; they do not replace a site assessment.

For example, note comfort concerns, available storage space and whether other trades will be working at the same time. Ask the installer which changes belong in the quotation.

Mention stairs, shared entrances, occupied rooms and times when the work area will be unavailable. Agree a practical access plan before work starts.

Compare the scope

Compare the full installation scope, rather than a headline boiler price. Keep allowances, assumptions and exclusions alongside the quotation.

Possible interfaces include electrical work, cabinetry, decoration and access panels. A written responsibility list helps prevent gaps between trades.

This may include the site assessment, equipment availability, access arrangements and the sequence of other renovation works. Record any conditions attached to the proposed dates.

Plan the working day and handover

Ask about the anticipated sequence of work, temporary interruptions and whom to contact if the schedule changes. Confirm the arrangements with the contractor rather than assuming every project follows the same timetable.

Ask what product instructions, installation records and warranty information will be supplied. Record the contact route for questions after completion.

A simple project folder makes the agreed scope easier to check. Record changes as they are agreed, including any effect on cost or programme.

Using this checklist

Take your notes to the site visit and use the same brief when comparing quotations. Keep unresolved items as questions for the contractor. Clear boundaries between included work, optional work and work by others are more useful than comparing prices without their assumptions.
